Over the past few months, I've been asking a lot of the older people I speak to in industry the same question:

*In a world where Claude can already do much of the work of a junior engineer or analyst, what will a fresh college graduate have to offer?*

The answer I get is almost always some variation of "you let me know when you figure that out" or "your generation is in quite an unfortunate position". Of course, they phrase it much more eloquently and gently than that, but the sentiment stands.

One portfolio manager at the hedge fund I interned at told me quite candidly that he simply saw no monetary reason to hire a new graduate analyst with the current state of things. He's someone whose advice I trust, and I appreciated that he was willing to say so plainly.[^1]

For a long time, being smart, technically competent, and willing to work hard was a pretty reliable way to make yourself useful. You'd enter an industry, learn from people more experienced than you, and gradually develop the judgment to contribute something of your own.

But what happens when much of the work that once made those qualities valuable can be done by a machine?

I'm less interested in predicting exactly which jobs will disappear than in understanding what this means for the next generation. If intelligence and technical ability become increasingly accessible to everyone, what will distinguish the people who go on to do meaningful work?

And this isn't just about entry-level jobs. If AI automates most research by, say, 2030, what does that mean for someone pursuing a PhD today?

In conversations with researchers, and increasingly in what I read online, I keep coming across the same idea of *research taste.*

## From Research Taste to Worldliness

Research taste seems to be the answer to everything these days.

AI can implement your ideas, run your experiments, and increasingly suggest directions of its own. But knowing which problems are worth pursuing, which results are interesting, and when to abandon an idea? That's where researchers still hope to distinguish themselves.

In other words, it's not just about being able to do the research. It's about knowing what research is worth doing.

And research taste isn't something you can simply learn from a textbook. You develop it through reading papers, running experiments, pursuing ideas that turn out to be dead ends, and gradually building an intuition for what makes a problem interesting.

I've been wondering whether there's a broader version of this idea that applies to life outside research, and I think the closest word for it is *worldliness*.

By worldliness, I mean an understanding of people and how the world works. An intuition for what people want, what frustrates them, what makes them happy, and what kinds of problems are actually worth solving.

Just as research taste comes from immersing yourself in research, worldliness comes from immersing yourself in the world.

Reading widely. Meeting people whose lives look nothing like yours. Traveling. Trying things you're terrible at. Spending time thinking about what you believe, what you care about, and why. Paying attention to the things around you instead of just moving through life on autopilot.

I've become increasingly convinced that much of what we call human ingenuity comes from connecting experiences that, on the surface, have very little to do with each other. You encounter something in one part of your life, face a problem in another, and suddenly recognize a connection that wasn't obvious before.

Of course, AI can make connections too, and there's a nontrivial chance it will eventually become better than us at developing research taste, or even something resembling worldliness.

But for now, I think there's increasing value in diversifying your firsthand experience of the world and developing your own perspective on it.

## What do young people have going for us?

One obvious advantage is that we've grown up with these tools. We're comfortable using AI to write code, build things, and learn. But I don't think that's a particularly durable edge. As the models improve, knowing how to use them will become less and less of a differentiator.

There's also something to be said for entering the world without decades of assumptions about how things are supposed to work. We're learning how the world works at the same time that the world itself is changing, and perhaps there's an advantage in that.

But I think the more important question is what we can offer beyond being comfortable with technology and willing to adapt.

For a long time, the minimum offering of a promising young person was something like: *"I'm smart, technically competent, and willing to work incredibly hard."*

It increasingly seems like this will no longer be enough. It feels like we now have to answer the harder question of "what can I bring to the table that isn't already available to everyone else?"

Maybe it's an unusually deep understanding of a problem. Maybe it's a particular combination of interests, a network of people, or a perspective shaped by experiences other people haven't had.

But finding that edge is hard, and historically, much of that discovery happened through doing relatively ordinary work. You'd spend years learning a craft, encountering problems, and gradually developing a perspective that was distinctly your own.

I wonder what happens when AI takes over the journey through which people once developed their judgment. I don't have a good answer to that problem, but it has changed how I think about spending my own time.

I still want to become technically excellent and still believe in working hard. But I also want to spend more time reading, writing, meeting interesting people, and pursuing things I'm genuinely curious about. 

Perhaps that's where worldliness comes in. The more we expose ourselves to the world, the more likely we discover what we uniquely have to contribute.

[^1]: I should probably acknowledge that my sample is heavily biased toward machine learning researchers, software engineers, and people in finance. I'm sure an aspiring plumber or chimney sweep would have a very different perspective on the future of work. But given the direction AI is heading, I suspect these questions won't remain confined to my little corner of the economy forever.
